01

Pulse

The sound

An echo image starts as one short pulse of sound. The scanner times each echo and changes the time into depth.

adult transthoracic, 2–5 MHz
1 MHz10 MHz
Cycles per pulse
Gain

Time becomes depth

The scanner assumes one speed of sound in all soft tissue: 1540 m/s. Depth is speed × time ÷ 2, because the pulse goes to the reflector and back. The round trip takes about 13 µs for each centimeter of depth, so an echo from 10 cm arrives after about 130 µs.

Wavelength sets the detail

wavelength = speed ÷ frequency  ·  axial resolution = cycles × wavelength ÷ 2

At 7 MHz the wavelength is about 0.22 mm. Axial resolution is half the length of the pulse, because the pulse makes a round trip. A higher frequency or fewer cycles give a shorter pulse, and two reflectors closer together show as two echoes.

Echoes come from steps in impedance

Acoustic impedance is density × speed of sound. At each boundary the reflected fraction of the intensity is ((Z2 − Z1) ÷ (Z2 + Z1))². Fat on muscle reflects about 1.4% of the intensity. Soft tissue on air reflects almost all of it: the lung stops the beam, and the image ends there.

Attenuation limits the depth

Tissue absorbs and scatters the pulse. The loss increases approximately in proportion to frequency. The textbook average for soft tissue is 0.5 dB per cm per MHz; blood loses much less (0.17 dB/cm at 1 MHz) and muscle more (1.2 dB/cm). The figure adds the loss layer by layer.

  • Adult transthoracic studies use 2.0 to 5.0 MHz.
  • ASE recommends the highest frequency that still reaches the depth of interest.
  • Time gain compensation amplifies late, deep echoes to offset attenuation. It amplifies the noise too.

Key points

  1. Depth comes from time: about 13 µs per centimeter, round trip.
  2. A higher frequency gives finer axial detail and less depth.
  3. Large impedance steps give bright echoes. Air and bone block the beam.
02

Image

The picture

A sector image is many scan lines, one after another. Depth, sector width and line density set how many frames the scanner can make each second.

Artifact

Frame rate

time per line ≈ 2 × depth ÷ c  ·  time per frame = lines × time per line

Each scan line waits for the echoes from the deepest point before the next pulse goes out. More lines, more depth or more focal zones make each frame slower. In the ASE example, 17 cm with a narrow sector gave 84 frames a second; 24 cm and a wide sector gave 43.

To increase the frame rate, decrease the depth, narrow the sector, use fewer focal zones or use write zoom. The heart moves fast, so temporal resolution is important.

Lateral resolution

Detail across the beam depends on the beam width. The beam is narrowest at the focus, and lateral resolution is typically 1 to 2 mm. Put the focus at the depth of the structure. More, closer lines give better lateral detail but a lower frame rate.

Artifacts

The scanner assumes that sound travels in a straight line, reflects once, comes only from the beam axis, and returns at a time proportional to depth. Each artifact breaks one of these assumptions.

  • Reverberation. Sound bounces between two strong reflectors. Copies appear at 2 and 3 times the distance.
  • Shadowing. A strong reflector or attenuator, such as a prosthetic ring or calcium, removes the echoes behind it.
  • Mirror. A strong reflector, often the pleura, places a copy of the structures in front of it behind it.
  • Side lobe. Off-axis energy from a strong reflector shows as if it came from the main beam, often as an arc.

Harmonic imaging forms the image from twice the transmit frequency, and ASE recommends it. It decreases side-lobe artifacts, but it can make valve leaflets look thicker.

Key points

  1. Frame rate = 1 ÷ (lines × time per line).
  2. Decrease depth and sector width to make the frame rate higher.
  3. Name the broken assumption to name the artifact.

Windows

The views

Each standard view is one plane through the heart. The probe position, the rotation of the marker and the tilt of the beam select the plane.

Window keys 1–5
View [ and ] step

Four windows

ASE describes four acoustic windows: parasternal, apical, subcostal and suprasternal notch. Parasternal and apical imaging is done with the patient on the left side; subcostal and suprasternal imaging with the patient supine.

  • Parasternal long axis. Third or fourth left intercostal space, marker toward the right shoulder, 9 to 10 o’clock.
  • Parasternal short axis. Rotate 90° clockwise from the long axis; the marker points to the left shoulder. Tilt the beam to step from the great vessels to the mitral valve, the papillary muscles and the apex.
  • Apical. At the apical impulse, marker at 4 to 5 o’clock. From the four-chamber view, rotate about 60° counterclockwise for the two-chamber view and 60° more for the three-chamber view. Tilt anteriorly for the five-chamber view.
  • Subcostal. Below the xiphoid. It shows the heart, the pericardium, the RV free wall and the inferior vena cava; angled a little to the patient's left, the abdominal aorta.
  • Suprasternal. It shows the ascending aorta, the arch, the head vessels and the descending aorta.

A good apical view

An apical view that is not foreshortened shows about two thirds LV and one third LA along the long axis. A plane that misses the true apex makes the LV look short and round, and its volumes read low.

The British Society of Echocardiography lists a minimum dataset of views in scanning order, and asks for at least two cardiac cycles for each clip.

Key points

  1. Parasternal long → short: rotate 90° clockwise.
  2. Apical four → two → three chamber: rotate about 60° counterclockwise each step.
  3. Measure the IVC 1 to 2 cm from the right atrium, in the subcostal view.

  • Each apical view shows two opposite walls of the LV, and together the three views cover the 17 segments.

Key points

  1. Apical planes rotate about the LV long axis.
  2. Short-axis planes are parallel slices.
  3. Every structure appears in several views: use at least two.
05

Cycle

One heartbeat

One heartbeat has four valve events. Echo times each event from the ECG, from valve motion and from Doppler.

R wavesystolediastolenext R

The Wiggers diagram

The diagram plots LV pressure, LV volume, LA pressure and aortic pressure against time, and marks the valve events. Mitral closure makes the first heart sound and aortic closure the second. Electrical events come before mechanical events: the QRS comes before contraction.

Intervals

In 1,969 healthy adults, isovolumic contraction lasted 40 ± 10 ms, ejection 292 ± 23 ms and isovolumic relaxation 96 ± 19 ms. The model uses these means at 70 beats a minute. Isovolumic relaxation becomes longer with age.

Timing on echo

  • End-diastole is the first frame after mitral valve closure, or the frame with the largest LV; the QRS or the R wave can stand in.
  • End-systole is the frame at or after aortic valve closure, near the end of the T wave.
  • Mitral inflow: the E wave peaks after the T wave; the A wave comes after the P wave.
  • IVRT runs from the end of LVOT flow to the start of mitral inflow. Record it with CW Doppler through the LVOT, from the apical five-chamber or long-axis view, so that both signals show.

A short IVRT (under 70 ms) in a patient with heart disease suggests a high LA pressure. IVRT is age-dependent, so do not use one normal value for all ages.

Early filling brings most of the blood into the LV; atrial contraction adds the rest, about one fifth in the example of Mitchell and Wang.

Key points

  1. S1 = mitral closure; S2 = aortic closure.
  2. End-diastole: first frame after mitral closure. End-systole: aortic closure.
  3. IVRT: end of LVOT flow to start of the E wave.
06

Loop

Pressure and volume

The pressure–volume loop shows one beat as a shape. Contractility, afterload and chamber stiffness move its corners, and the ejection fraction follows them.

Heart

The four sides

The loop runs counterclockwise: isovolumic contraction, ejection, isovolumic relaxation and filling. The width of the loop is the stroke volume; its area is the stroke work.

Three lines

  • ESPVR. The end-systolic points lie on a near-straight line, P = Ees × (V − V0). Its slope, Ees, is a relatively load-independent measure of contractility.
  • EDPVR. The end-diastolic relation is curved, close to exponential, and shows the passive stiffness of the chamber.
  • Ea. Effective arterial elastance is end-systolic pressure ÷ stroke volume: the slope of the line from the EDV on the volume axis to the end-systolic point.

Coupling and EF

EF = Ees ÷ (Ees + Ea)  (with V0 taken as 0)

EF is a ratio of the heart and its load, not a pure measure of contractility. A higher afterload decreases EF even when contractility does not change. Stroke work is largest when Ea equals Ees; efficiency is highest at a lower Ea/Ees. At rest, healthy adults have an Ea/Ees of about 0.5 to 1.0, depending on the method.

Without a catheter, end-systolic pressure is often estimated as 0.9 × the systolic cuff pressure.

The presets

  • Weak ventricle. Low Ees: the ventricle dilates and ejects a small fraction.
  • Stiff ventricle. Steep EDPVR: filling pressure is high at a normal volume, and EF stays normal.
  • Pressure load. High Ea, as with aortic stenosis or hypertension: the loop is tall.

Preload is the stretch of the muscle before contraction; afterload is the load against which the ventricle ejects.

Key points

  1. Ees (slope of the ESPVR) indexes contractility.
  2. Ea = end-systolic pressure ÷ stroke volume.
  3. EF depends on both: Ees ÷ (Ees + Ea).
07

Shift

The Doppler effect

Doppler measures blood velocity from the frequency shift of the echoes. The beam angle and the Nyquist limit decide what the scanner can report.

Mode

The Doppler equation

Δf = 2 × f0 × v × cos θ ÷ c

Flow toward the transducer gives a positive shift, and flow away a negative shift. The equation uses c = 1540 m/s. With blood at about 1 m/s and MHz transducers, the shift is a few kHz: an audible sound.

The angle

Cardiac Doppler assumes the beam is parallel to the flow (θ = 0). A 20° angle underestimates the velocity by only about 6%. At 60° the scanner reports half the true velocity (cos 60° = 0.5, a calculation). ASE does not recommend angle correction: align the beam with the flow, use several windows, and report the highest velocity.

Pulsed wave and the Nyquist limit

Pulsed-wave Doppler measures at one depth, but it must wait for each echo before the next pulse. The deepest sample sets the pulse repetition frequency (PRF), and the highest velocity without aliasing is c² ÷ (8 × depth × f0). The Nyquist limit is PRF ÷ 2; faster flow wraps to the other side of the baseline.

  • Move the sample volume closer, or use a lower Doppler frequency, to increase the limit.
  • High-PRF Doppler adds sample volumes to increase the limit, and accepts range ambiguity.
  • Continuous wave has no Nyquist limit, and no depth information.

Color Doppler

Color shows the mean velocity at many sample volumes: red toward the transducer, blue away. It is a pulsed method, so it aliases too. ASE suggests a scale of 50 to 70 cm/s for routine color, and about 30 cm/s for slow flow. A small, shallow color box keeps the frame rate high.

A wide spectrum does not always mean turbulent flow; a large sample volume or high gain also widen it.

Key points

  1. Measured velocity = true velocity × cos θ.
  2. Deeper sample, higher frequency: lower Nyquist limit.
  3. For high velocities, use continuous wave.
08

Pressure

Flow and gradients

Two Doppler calculations appear in almost every report: stroke volume from the LV outflow tract, and RV systolic pressure from the tricuspid jet.

IVC collapse with a sniff

Stroke volume

SV = 0.785 × d² × VTI  (d, VTI in cm; SV in mL)

The outflow tract is taken as a circle with diameter d. Measure d in a zoomed parasternal long-axis view, inner edge to inner edge, in mid-systole. Record the LVOT velocity with pulsed wave from the apical five-chamber or long-axis view, just below the valve. Take both at the same level.

The diameter is squared, so a small error becomes a large one. Repeated LVOT measurements vary by 5 to 8%, the largest error in the continuity equation. A 2 mm error on a 2.0 cm outflow tract changes the stroke volume by about 20%.

Normal resting values from catheter studies: stroke volume 60 to 100 mL, cardiac output 4 to 8 L/min, cardiac index 2.5 to 4.0 L/min/m².

Pressure from velocity

ΔP = 4 v²  ·  RVSP = 4 × (peak TR velocity)² + RA pressure

The simplified Bernoulli equation gives the pressure difference in mmHg from a velocity in m/s. It holds when the velocity before the narrowing is 1.5 m/s or less. The TR jet gives the pressure difference between the RV and the RA; add the RA pressure from the IVC.

  • IVC ≤2.1 cm that collapses ≥50% with a sniff: RA pressure 3 mmHg.
  • IVC >2.1 cm that collapses <50%: 15 mmHg. Other combinations: 8 mmHg.
  • Report one number, not a range.

A resting peak TR velocity of 2.9 m/s or more, or 2.8 m/s with two other signs, suggests pulmonary hypertension.

Key points

  1. SV = area × VTI; the diameter error is squared.
  2. ΔP = 4v².
  3. RVSP = 4 × TR² + RA pressure from the IVC.

Eject

Systolic function

LVEF is the fraction of the end-diastolic volume that the LV ejects. Measure it from biplane volumes; the right ventricle needs its own measures.

Ventricle
Frame

Biplane volumes

The biplane method of disks (modified Simpson’s rule) is the recommended 2D method; use 3D when it is available and the images are good. Trace the LV at end-diastole and end-systole in the apical four- and two-chamber views. The software divides the LV into a stack of thin disks and adds their volumes. Do not calculate volumes from linear dimensions.

Foreshortened views make the volumes read low. In the figure, both volumes fall by the same fraction, so the EF changes little while the volumes are wrong.

Normal values

  • LVEF normal: 52 to 72% in men, 54 to 74% in women. Mildly abnormal: 41 to 51% (men) or 41 to 53% (women); moderately 30 to 40%; severely below 30%.
  • Heart failure categories by LVEF: reduced ≤40%, mildly reduced 41 to 49%, preserved ≥50%.
  • Global longitudinal strain: normal more negative than −18%, borderline −16 to −18%, abnormal less negative than −16%.

Report one LVEF, with its method. On serial 2D studies of stable patients, EF varied by more than 10 points; non-contrast 3D varied by about 6.

The right ventricle

ASE grades three simple measures of RV systolic function:

  • TAPSE. M-mode excursion of the lateral tricuspid annulus toward the apex, in the RV-focused four-chamber view. Normal above 1.7 cm.
  • S′. Tissue Doppler velocity of the same point. Normal above 9.5 cm/s.
  • Fractional area change. (end-diastolic area − end-systolic area) ÷ end-diastolic area. Normal above 35%.

Key points

  1. Biplane disks in two apical views; avoid foreshortening.
  2. Normal LVEF starts at 52% (men) and 54% (women).
  3. Report at least one measure of RV function.

Segments

Regional function

The LV wall has 17 segments. A coronary artery usually supplies one set of segments, so the pattern of abnormal motion points to the artery.

Names and numbers

Start at the anterior junction of the septum and the RV free wall and go counterclockwise: anteroseptal, inferoseptal, inferior, inferolateral, anterolateral, anterior. The basal and mid levels have six segments each, the apical level four, and the apical cap is segment 17.

  • Use 16 segments to score wall motion, and 17 for perfusion or to compare with other modalities.
  • Score each segment: 1 normal or hyperkinetic, 2 hypokinetic, 3 akinetic, 4 dyskinetic. The 2025 reporting guideline adds 5, aneurysmal. In the figure, tap a segment on the bull's-eye or on an apical view, or use its numbered button, to move it to the next score.
  • The wall motion score index is the mean of the scores; 1.00 is normal.

Views and walls

The apical four-chamber view shows the inferoseptal and anterolateral walls; the two-chamber view the inferior and anterior walls; the three-chamber view the inferolateral and anteroseptal walls.

Territories

In the standard map, the LAD supplies segments 1, 2, 7, 8, 13, 14 and 17; the right coronary artery 3, 4, 9, 10 and 15; the circumflex 5, 6, 11, 12 and 16. Supply varies: in one CT study, 72% of patients had at least one segment supplied by a different artery. Treat the map as a guide, not a rule.

Regional strain values are not recommended for clinical decisions, because they vary between tests and vendors.

Key points

  1. 16 segments for wall motion; the index is their mean score.
  2. Each apical view shows two opposite walls.
  3. Anterior wall, septum and apex: think LAD.

Fill

Diastolic function

Diastolic function decides how high the LA pressure must be to fill the LV. As the LV relaxes more slowly and then stiffens, the inflow, the annulus, the pulmonary veins and the LA change together; the 2025 ASE algorithm reads them.

MeasurementValueAbnormal ifResult

As the grade rises

  1. Grade 1. Relaxation slows: e′ falls, and E/A falls to 0.8 or less. E/e′ and the PA pressure stay normal, so the LA pressure is normal.
  2. Grade 2. The LA pressure rises (mild to moderate) and E/A stays below 2: the inflow can look normal again, a pseudonormal pattern. E/e′ and the TR velocity rise; pulmonary vein S/D falls to 0.67 or less; LA reservoir strain falls to 18% or less.
  3. Grade 3. A marked rise in LA pressure with E/A ≥2, the restrictive pattern. With a normal EF, a restrictive pattern with a dilated LA carries a poor prognosis.

The values on the page are illustrative. With a normal EF, S/D can stay above 0.67 although the filling pressures are high; LA strain ≤18% is specific but can miss raised pressure.

What the signals mean

  • Mitral E and A. Early filling (E) and atrial filling (A). Slow relaxation decreases early filling relative to late filling, so E/A falls.
  • e′. The tissue Doppler velocity of the mitral annulus in early diastole. It depends directly on LV relaxation, so it is the first value in the algorithm.
  • E/e′. Rises with LA pressure. In an early study, lateral E/e′ tracked wedge pressure (r = 0.87).
  • TR velocity. Without lung or pulmonary vascular disease, a peak of 2.8 m/s or more supports a raised LA pressure.

The 2025 algorithm, sinus rhythm

  1. Check three values: e′ reduced (septal ≤6, lateral ≤7 or average ≤6.5 cm/s); E/e′ raised (septal ≥15, lateral ≥13 or average ≥14); TR ≥2.8 m/s, or PA systolic pressure ≥35 mmHg when the RA pressure is known.
  2. All three normal: normal LA pressure and normal diastolic function.
  3. Only e′ reduced and E/A ≤0.8: normal LA pressure, grade 1.
  4. One or two abnormal (other cases): check pulmonary vein S/D ≤0.67, LA reservoir strain ≤18% or LA volume index >34 mL/m². One or more abnormal: raised LA pressure. None: normal LA pressure.
  5. All three abnormal: raised LA pressure.
  6. With raised LA pressure: E/A <2 is grade 2; E/A ≥2 is grade 3.

In a validation study of 951 patients with catheter pressures, the algorithm left 2 patients unclassified (the 2016 approach left 38) and was 86% accurate.

Where it does not apply

Do not use it in atrial fibrillation, mitral stenosis, severe primary mitral regurgitation, moderate or severe annular calcification, after mitral repair or replacement, after heart transplant, with an LV assist device, with pericardial constriction, or with pulmonary hypertension of non-cardiac cause. The guideline does not apply to children, to normal pregnancy, or during surgery.

If the grade is uncertain, report whether the LA pressure is normal or raised. e′ decreases with age; age-adjusted limits can replace the fixed cut-offs.

In advanced cardiac amyloidosis, the annular s′, e′ and a′ can all fall below 5 cm/s: the “5-5-5” sign.

Key points

  1. First step: e′, E/e′, TR velocity.
  2. Second step, if needed: pulmonary vein S/D, LA strain, LA volume.
  3. Grade from E/A once the LA pressure is raised.

Aortic stenosis

Pressure load

As a calcified aortic valve narrows, the jet speeds up and the LV wall thickens against the pressure. Late, the LV fails: the stroke volume falls, and the gradient falls with it.

What changes, in order

  1. Progressive stenosis. Mild: peak velocity 2.0–2.9 m/s or mean gradient below 20 mmHg. Moderate: 3.0–3.9 m/s or 20–39 mmHg. Early LV diastolic dysfunction can be present; the LVEF is normal.
  2. Severe stenosis. Peak velocity ≥4 m/s or mean gradient ≥40 mmHg; the area is typically ≤1.0 cm². The LV shows diastolic dysfunction and mild hypertrophy, with a normal LVEF. Very severe: ≥5 m/s or ≥60 mmHg.
  3. LV dysfunction. Severe stenosis with an LVEF below 50%.
  4. Low flow, low gradient. The failing LV cannot open the valve fully: area ≤1.0 cm² with a velocity below 4 m/s or a mean gradient below 40 mmHg.

The letter stages also need the symptoms: stage C has none, stage D has them. The page follows the echo findings only.

Grading

AVA = LVOT area × VTI(LVOT) ÷ VTI(AV)  ·  mean gradient = mean of 4v² over ejection

  • Find the highest jet velocity with continuous wave from several windows, and report the window; do not use angle correction.
  • Any one of peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s, mean gradient ≥40 mmHg or area <1.0 cm² suggests severe stenosis; ideally all three agree. A velocity ratio below 0.25 also indicates severe stenosis.
  • With more severe obstruction the peak comes later in systole and the curve is more rounded; mild stenosis peaks early, with a triangular curve.
  • At normal flow, a mean gradient of 40 mmHg matches an area nearer 0.8 than 1.0 cm². The “area” band on the slider is this gap.

When the gradient falls

  • A stroke volume index below 35 mL/m² is low flow.
  • Low EF. Area <1.0 cm², mean gradient <40 mmHg, LVEF <50%: classical low-flow, low-gradient stenosis. Low-dose dobutamine: a velocity ≥4.0 m/s with an area that stays ≤1.0 cm² confirms severe stenosis; an area that rises above 1.0 cm² suggests pseudo-severe stenosis.
  • Normal EF. A small LV with a low stroke volume can give a low gradient through a severe stenosis: paradoxical low flow.

Key points

  1. Severe: 4 m/s, 40 mmHg, 1.0 cm², ideally all three.
  2. The LV thickens first; the EF falls late.
  3. A falling gradient with a falling EF is not a better valve: check the flow.
13

Mitral stenosis

Inflow obstruction

In rheumatic mitral stenosis the commissures fuse and the leaflets dome. As the orifice narrows, the LA pressure rises: the LA enlarges, the pulmonary pressure rises and the right heart follows.

What changes, in order

  1. Progressive stenosis. Commissural fusion and diastolic doming; area >1.5 cm², pressure half-time <150 ms; mild to moderate LA enlargement; normal pulmonary pressure at rest.
  2. Severe stenosis. Area ≤1.5 cm², pressure half-time ≥150 ms; severe LA enlargement; PA systolic pressure above 50 mmHg.

ASE divides progressive stenosis into mild (area >2.5 cm², half-time <100 ms, mean gradient <5 mmHg, PA systolic pressure <30 mmHg) and moderate (2.5–1.6 cm², 100–149 ms, 5–9 mmHg, 30–49 mmHg).

Measuring the area

MVA = 220 ÷ pressure half-time

  • Planimetry. The short axis shows the commissural fusion and allows planimetry of the orifice. When the methods disagree, planimetry is the reference.
  • Pressure half-time. The time for the peak gradient to fall by half; the velocity falls to about 70% of its peak. The constant 220 is empirical; Hatle described the method in 1979. The half-time is not reliable just after balloon valvotomy, with severe aortic regurgitation, or with abnormal LV compliance.
  • Mean gradient. It changes with heart rate and flow, so it is not a severity criterion; report it with the heart rate. Move the heart rate: the same valve gives a higher gradient at a faster rate.

Key points

  1. Severe: area ≤1.5 cm² and half-time ≥150 ms.
  2. The LA and the pulmonary pressure carry the consequence.
  3. Report the gradient with the heart rate.

Mitral regurgitation

Volume load, left heart

A leaking mitral valve sends part of each stroke back into the LA. The LV and LA dilate to carry the extra volume; late, the LV fails.

What changes, in order

  1. Mild. A small central jet, normal leaflets, a vena contracta below 0.3 cm, no flow convergence and A-wave-dominant inflow: the regurgitation is mild.
  2. Moderate. The LV and LA are normal or mildly dilated; pulmonary vein flow is normal or blunted in systole.
  3. Severe. EROA ≥0.40 cm², regurgitant volume ≥60 mL, regurgitant fraction ≥50%, vena contracta ≥0.7 cm. The LV and LA dilate; the CW signal is dense and triangular; systolic flow in the pulmonary veins stops or reverses; mitral E exceeds 1.2 m/s.
  4. LV dysfunction. ACC/AHA stage C1: LVEF >60% and LV end-systolic diameter <40 mm. Stage C2: LVEF ≤60% or diameter ≥40 mm.

The PISA method

flow = 2π r² × Va  ·  EROA = flow ÷ peak velocity  ·  RVol = EROA × VTI

Blood speeds up as it converges on the orifice; at the aliasing velocity Va the color changes, and that shell is a hemisphere of radius r. Shift the baseline toward the jet to lower Va to about 30 to 40 cm/s; measure the radius at the time of the peak jet velocity. A radius of 1.0 cm or more at this setting is specific for severe MR.

  • The radius is squared: errors of 10 to 25% are common, even among experts.
  • An elliptical orifice, common in secondary MR, makes PISA underestimate the flow. In secondary MR, an EROA ≥0.30 cm² by 2D PISA may still be severe; EACVI accepts ≥0.30 cm² or ≥45 mL.
  • For regurgitation in only part of systole, use the regurgitant volume; one frame overestimates the EROA.

Pitfalls

  • A normal LA size generally excludes severe chronic MR; a dilated LA alone does not prove it.
  • Color jet area helps to exclude MR but is unreliable to grade it; set the color scale to 50 to 70 cm/s for jets.
  • Systolic flow reversal in the pulmonary veins is specific for severe regurgitation but not sensitive.

Key points

  1. Severe: EROA ≥0.40 cm², volume ≥60 mL, fraction ≥50%.
  2. Support it with E ≥1.2 m/s and pulmonary vein reversal.
  3. LVEF ≤60% or end-systolic diameter ≥40 mm: stage C2.

Aortic regurgitation

Volume and pressure load

A leaking aortic valve returns blood to the LV in diastole. The LV receives blood from the LA and from the aorta, dilates, and ejects a large stroke volume; late, it fails.

What changes, in order

  1. Mild. A small central jet, a faint or incomplete CW signal, a pressure half-time above 500 ms and a brief early diastolic reversal in the descending aorta.
  2. Moderate. The LV is normal or dilated; half-time 500–200 ms; regurgitant volume 30–59 mL.
  3. Severe. Vena contracta >0.6 cm, jet width ≥65% of the LVOT, regurgitant volume ≥60 mL, fraction ≥50%, EROA ≥0.30 cm², half-time below 200 ms, holodiastolic reversal. Holodiastolic reversal in the abdominal aorta is consistent with severe AR. EACVI uses an end-diastolic velocity ≥20 cm/s in the proximal descending aorta. Chronic severe AR also requires LV dilation.
  4. LV dysfunction. ACC/AHA stage C1: LVEF >55% and end-systolic diameter <50 mm. Stage C2: LVEF ≤55%, or diameter >50 mm or >25 mm/m².

Why the LVOT velocity rises

The LV ejects the forward volume plus the volume that returns in diastole. The regurgitant volume is the stroke volume through the leaking valve minus the stroke volume through a competent valve. On the page, the LVOT VTI rises as the leak grows.

Pitfalls

  • The half-time shortens as the LV diastolic pressure rises and can lengthen in chronic adaptation to severe AR.
  • The length of the jet into the LV is not a reliable measure of severity.

Key points

  1. Severe: vena contracta >0.6 cm, jet ≥65% of the LVOT, volume ≥60 mL.
  2. Holodiastolic reversal in the abdominal aorta: severe.
  3. LVEF ≤55% or end-systolic diameter >50 mm: stage C2.

Tricuspid regurgitation

Volume load, right heart

A leaking tricuspid valve loads the right heart. The RV, the annulus and the RA dilate and the veins fill; at the torrential end the jet slows, because the RV and RA pressures come close.

What changes, in order

  1. Mild. A small, narrow central jet; a faint CW signal; systolic dominance in the hepatic veins; IVC below 2 cm.
  2. Moderate. The RV and RA are normal or mildly dilated; the hepatic veins show systolic blunting; vena contracta 0.3–0.69 cm; EROA 0.20–0.39 cm².
  3. Severe. Vena contracta ≥0.7 cm, EROA ≥0.40 cm², regurgitant volume ≥45 mL, PISA radius >0.9 cm (baseline shifted to 28 cm/s), jet area >10 cm²; systolic reversal in the hepatic veins; tricuspid E >1.0 m/s; IVC >2.5 cm. ACC/AHA adds a dilated RV and RA and a raised RA pressure with a c-V wave.
  4. Beyond severe. Massive: vena contracta 14–20 mm, EROA 60–79 mm², volume 60–74 mL. Torrential: ≥21 mm, ≥80 mm², ≥75 mL. A report can name them as subgroups of severe TR.

Pitfalls

  • The TR velocity does not show the regurgitant volume. Very severe TR often has a low velocity, about 2 m/s, because the RV and RA pressures equalize; EACVI gives a peak below 2 m/s in massive TR.
  • Hepatic vein systolic reversal is specific for severe TR, after other causes (atrial fibrillation, pacing, RA or RV compliance) are excluded.
  • The volume limit for severe TR (45 mL) is lower than for MR (60 mL) at the same EROA, because the TR velocity is lower.
  • At left-sided valve surgery, a tricuspid annulus above 4.0 cm in diastole makes tricuspid repair reasonable, even with less than severe TR.

Key points

  1. Severe: vena contracta ≥0.7 cm, EROA ≥0.40 cm², volume ≥45 mL.
  2. Hepatic vein systolic reversal is specific.
  3. A low jet velocity does not exclude severe TR.

Pericardium

Effusion and tamponade

Fluid in the pericardial sac shows as an echo-free space round the heart. As it collects, the pressure in the sac rises; when it reaches the pressure in the right heart, the chambers cannot fill: tamponade.

What changes, in order

  1. None. The normal sac holds 10–50 mL of fluid. A separation seen only in systole is a trivial effusion; a separation in systole and diastole means more than 50 mL.
  2. Mild (small). An echo-free space below 10 mm, measured at end-diastole between the epicardium and the parietal pericardium. ASE gives 50–100 mL for a small effusion and calls the volumes arbitrary.
  3. Moderate. 10–20 mm, about 100–500 mL. In this model the pressure in the sac stays below the pressure in the right heart, so no chamber collapses.
  4. Severe (large). More than 20 mm; ASE adds very large, more than 25 mm. The heart can swing in the fluid. ESC links the swing to electrical alternans on the ECG.
  5. Tamponade. The pressure in the sac reaches the pressure in the right heart. A chamber collapses in its relaxation phase, when its pressure falls below the pressure in the sac.

Signs of tamponade

  • Right atrium. The wall folds in near the peak of the R wave. It stays in until filling in ventricular systole raises the atrial pressure. Collapse for more than a third of the cycle is nearly 100% sensitive and specific. In the first study, a collapse time above 0.34 of the cycle gave 94% sensitivity and 100% specificity.
  • Right ventricle. The free wall collapses in early diastole, after the T wave. This occurs when cardiac output is about 20% lower but blood pressure has not fallen.
  • Left atrium. The LA also collapses in about a quarter of patients; this sign is highly specific.
  • IVC. Wider than 2.1 cm, with less than 50% collapse in inspiration. This occurs in more than 90% of patients, but many other heart diseases also cause it. ESC 2025 uses 20 mm: sensitivity 97%, specificity 40%.
  • Filling with breathing. With inspiration, mitral E falls more than 25–30% and tricuspid E increases more than 40–60%. The RV gets larger and the LV smaller in inspiration, and the reverse occurs in expiration.
  • Hepatic veins. On the first beat of expiration, diastolic flow falls or reverses.

Pitfalls

  • Size does not define tamponade. The rate of accumulation matters more than the volume. Fluid that collects fast, as in haemopericardium, can cause tamponade at 200–300 mL; a slow effusion can reach 1–2 L first.
  • Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis: history, symptoms and signs, with echo confirmation. Pulsus paradoxus is an inspiratory fall in systolic pressure of more than 10 mmHg.
  • A brief RA inversion occurs without tamponade, so measure its duration. An M-mode line through the wall shows the timing best.
  • No chamber collapse gives a negative predictive value above 90%. RV hypertrophy, severe pulmonary hypertension or severe LV dysfunction can delay or prevent collapse.
  • Pericardial or pleural fluid: in the parasternal long axis, pericardial fluid lies between the descending aorta and the heart. Pleural fluid runs behind the aorta.
  • Respiratory variation of inflow is not a criterion alone. Under positive-pressure ventilation it is minimal, even in tamponade.

Key points

  1. End-diastolic echo-free space: mild <10 mm, moderate 10–20 mm, severe (large) >20 mm.
  2. RA collapse for more than a third of the cycle and RV collapse in early diastole: tamponade physiology.
  3. The rate of accumulation, not the size, sets the tolerance. Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis.

The study comes together

Order

  1. Demographics, essential history, the indication and the priority go at the top.
  2. The summary statement comes next, in every report.
  3. Then one heading for each structure: LV, septum, RV, LA, atrial septum, RA, the four valves, aorta, pulmonary artery, IVC, SVC, pericardium.
  4. Under each heading, structure first, then function; keep the Doppler findings with their structure.

The summary statement

It names five things: LV and RV function; significant valve disease, or none; other important positive findings; pertinent negatives for the indication; and a comparison with prior studies. Relate it to the indication, so the referring clinician can decide the next step. Label critical findings, put them first, and record who received them.

Words and numbers

  • Call every structure and number normal or abnormal. Grade only where guidelines grade.
  • Write “trace” for less than mild regurgitation. Avoid “mild-to-moderate” unless you explain why.
  • Give one LVEF, with its method.
  • Pressures, volumes and EF as whole numbers; velocity, area and length to 0.1.
  • Numbers and words must agree: “normal LA size” with an LA volume index of 50 mL/m² is an error.
  • State image quality: excellent, good, fair, poor or non-diagnostic. Measure the blood pressure at the bedside at the start of the study.

Accreditation

IAC requires standard reports across a laboratory. Routine inpatient studies must be read within 24 hours, outpatient studies by the end of the next business day, and STAT findings at once. Every structure that is not well seen must be noted, and the text must agree with the numbers.
